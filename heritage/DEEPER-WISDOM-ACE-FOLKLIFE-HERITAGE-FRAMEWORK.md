# Deeper Wisdom — ACE Folklife and Heritage Framework

**Public working edition:** November 26, 2026  
**Human authority:** Theresa J. Morris  
**Project home:** HIKIWIKI / ACE Folklife Historical Society  
**Guiding principle:** Wisdom Above Mechanism  
**Institutional boundary:** This is an independent project framework informed by public UNESCO concepts. It does not claim UNESCO affiliation, recognition, certification, inscription, or endorsement.

## Purpose

Deeper Wisdom is a heritage framework for preserving the knowledge carried by people, communities, places, records, creative traditions, and lived experience. It gives ACE Folklife Historical Society a clear way to organize historical accounts without confusing memory with proof, testimony with corroboration, or project canon with established science.

The framework joins four responsibilities:

1. **Receive the living account.**
2. **Preserve the original voice and context.**
3. **Research and label what can be verified.**
4. **Return the record to the community in an accessible form.**

## UNESCO-aligned heritage categories

UNESCO’s public heritage frameworks provide useful reference categories. ACE uses them as orientation, not as a claim of institutional status.

### 1. Oral traditions and expressions

Includes spoken histories, storytelling, names, proverbs, poetry, recitation, legends, language, and community vocabulary.

**ACE examples:** family accounts, veteran recollections, radio stories, Wordology records, local legends, contact narratives, and intergenerational teachings.

### 2. Performing arts

Includes music, dance, theatre, ceremony, recitation, and other enacted forms.

**ACE examples:** broadcasts, community programs, storytelling performances, songs, dramatic readings, and ceremonial presentations.

### 3. Social practices, rituals, and festive events

Includes shared customs and events through which communities express belonging and continuity.

**ACE examples:** reunions, commemorations, watch gatherings, holiday practices, organizational ceremonies, community service, and recurring shows.

### 4. Knowledge and practices concerning nature and the universe

Includes knowledge communities develop through relationships with land, sky, seasons, plants, animals, agriculture, navigation, healing, and cosmology.

**ACE examples:** skywatching traditions, environmental observations, agricultural or maritime knowledge, community cosmologies, and symbolic Cosmos Expanse narratives. Scientific claims remain separately evaluated.

### 5. Traditional craftsmanship

Includes the knowledge and skills involved in making and maintaining cultural objects.

**ACE examples:** bookmaking, textile work, art, instruments, tools, archival preparation, handmade objects, and community demonstrations.

### 6. Tangible cultural heritage

Includes monuments, groups of buildings, sites, artifacts, collections, and cultural landscapes whose material form carries historical meaning.

**ACE examples:** homes, studios, meeting places, memorial sites, printed books, photographs, uniforms, recordings, equipment, and organizational artifacts.

### 7. Natural heritage

Includes significant physical, biological, geological, ecological, and landscape features.

**ACE examples:** locally important landscapes, habitats, waterways, geological features, and the community histories attached to them. UNESCO World Heritage status is never implied without formal inscription.

### 8. Documentary heritage

Includes preserved analogue or digital content and its carrier: manuscripts, books, photographs, recordings, film, correspondence, websites, databases, and born-digital files.

**ACE examples:** show archives, interviews, ledgers, books, websites, email records, photographs, audio, video, agent registries, and dated Markdown reports.

### 9. Diversity of cultural expressions

Includes the conditions that allow people and communities to create, communicate, encounter, and sustain varied cultural expressions.

**ACE examples:** independent publishing, public-access media, multi-generational storytelling, community education, cross-cultural dialogue, and human-reviewed AI-assisted creative work.

## ACE Folklife Historical Society collection categories

### A. People and life histories

Biographies, autobiographical accounts, family histories, elders, founders, creators, workers, teachers, veterans, community leaders, and named AI-era project roles.

### B. Family, kinship, and community

Households, migration, neighborhoods, mutual aid, clubs, friendships, reunions, care networks, and intergenerational relationships.

### C. Veterans, public service, and civic memory

Military service, civil service, emergency response, public safety, communications work, community institutions, ceremonies, and remembrance.

### D. Work, enterprise, and technology

Occupations, small businesses, communications, transportation, publishing, computing, broadcasting, internet culture, and changes in tools and labor.

### E. Media, publishing, and performance

Books, newsletters, podcasts, radio, television, livestreams, public speaking, music, art, theatre, and community programming.

### F. Spiritual, metaphysical, and cosmological traditions

Beliefs, teachings, sacred narratives, rituals, visionary experiences, symbolic systems, and cosmological storytelling. These are attributed to their communities or narrators and are not presented as scientific findings without evidence.

### G. UFO, UAP, contact, and anomalous-experience accounts

First-person reports, investigative files, media history, community interpretation, and corroborating or conflicting evidence. Testimony, hypothesis, documented fact, and creative interpretation remain separately labeled.

### H. AI, cyberspace, and digital culture

Human interaction with AI systems, agent names and roles, online communities, platform history, digital collaboration, emerging language, and the preservation of born-digital culture.

### I. Place, landscape, and environment

Homes, towns, regions, travel routes, waterways, skies, habitats, sacred or meaningful places, and environmental change.

### J. Foodways, crafts, and everyday practice

Recipes, meals, textiles, repairs, collecting, household knowledge, making, celebrations, and ordinary practices that sustain community life.

### K. Organizations and movements

ACO, AISI, ACE, ACIR, Aletheon, clubs, associations, publishing networks, research groups, and their changing structures, missions, and records.

### L. Historical chronology and turning points

Foundings, renamings, migrations, launches, broadcasts, publications, discoveries, disputes, corrections, closures, and succession records.

## Deeper Wisdom STEM Program

**STEM means Science, Technology, Engineering, and Mathematics.**

The Deeper Wisdom STEM Program uses heritage as a real-world learning environment. Participants investigate a community question, preserve its human context, work with observable evidence, and produce something that can be checked and used.

This is a community education framework. It does not claim school accreditation, UNESCO sponsorship, or automatic scientific validation of every heritage account.

### STEM purpose

- Teach careful observation without dismissing lived experience.
- Turn archives and community questions into responsible research projects.
- Build technical and quantitative skills through meaningful local material.
- Help learners distinguish data, testimony, hypothesis, interpretation, and conclusion.
- Design useful preservation tools while protecting consent, privacy, and cultural rights.

### The four STEM lenses

| STEM lens | Heritage application | Core questions |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Science** | Observation, classification, hypothesis, testing, environmental study, conservation science | What can be observed? What evidence would support or challenge the claim? |
| **Technology** | Audio, video, scanning, databases, metadata, GIS, websites, AI assistance, digital preservation | Which tool captures and preserves the record without distorting it? |
| **Engineering** | Archive design, recording systems, storage, restoration, exhibits, sensors, accessibility, resilient backups | How can we build a reliable, safe, repairable system? |
| **Mathematics** | Measurement, timelines, statistics, geometry, scale, uncertainty, networks, mapping, checksums | What can be counted or measured, and what uncertainty must remain visible? |

### Program learning cycle

**Ask → Obtain consent → Observe → Preserve the original → Measure → Research → Model or build → Test → Interpret → Community review → Publish or restrict → Maintain**

Each stage has a different responsibility. A measurement does not replace a person’s account, and a compelling account does not replace measurement.

### Program strands

1. **Archive Science** — materials, deterioration, conservation conditions, file integrity, and media migration.
2. **Oral History Data** — ethical interviewing, transcription, coding, uncertainty, qualitative and quantitative comparison.
3. **Heritage Technology** — recording, scanning, metadata, databases, GIS, accessible websites, and responsible AI assistance.
4. **Preservation Engineering** — storage systems, backup design, exhibits, restoration tools, sensors, and disaster planning.
5. **Mathematics of Memory** — chronology, scale, geometry, networks, statistics, sampling, error, and confidence.
6. **Earth and Sky Heritage** — ecology, geology, weather, navigation, astronomy, and community knowledge of nature and the universe.
7. **Digital Culture and AI History** — repositories, model and platform history, human-AI collaboration records, provenance, permissions, and continuity.
8. **Community Innovation** — use STEM to solve an identified preservation or access problem for a real community.

### Sample projects

- Record an elder’s life history, produce a consent record and transcript, then map places and dates with uncertainty clearly marked.
- Compare an oral account with newspapers, photographs, service records, weather data, or public archives without treating disagreement as automatic disproof.
- Digitize photographs and calculate resolution, storage needs, checksums, and backup capacity.
- Document a local craft through materials analysis, measurement, process diagrams, and a narrated demonstration.
- Build a low-cost temperature and humidity monitor for a small archive.
- Map environmental or sky observations using time, location, direction, instrument limits, and alternative explanations.
- Create a public exhibit that separates source, testimony, measurement, interpretation, and project canon.
- Audit an AI-assisted historical summary against its original sources and record every correction.

### Learning outcomes

Participants should be able to:

- formulate a researchable question;
- obtain and document consent;
- distinguish qualitative and quantitative data;
- preserve an original source and its provenance;
- select appropriate tools and document their limits;
- calculate and communicate measurement uncertainty;
- build and test a practical preservation solution;
- evaluate competing explanations;
- explain what is verified, unverified, disputed, or interpretive;
- present results accessibly to the originating community.

### Data and mathematics rules

- Record units, instruments, dates, time zones, and methods.
- Retain raw data separately from cleaned or interpreted data.
- Never invent missing values.
- Report uncertainty, sample limitations, and possible bias.
- Do not use statistics to imply certainty beyond the evidence.
- Protect personal and culturally restricted data.
- Publish enough method for another person to understand or reproduce the calculation when ethical and permitted.

### Program record

Every STEM project should produce:

1. a question and community purpose;
2. consent and access conditions;
3. source and provenance register;
4. observation or data plan;
5. method, tools, and units;
6. raw and processed materials;
7. calculations, code, diagrams, or build notes;
8. results with uncertainty;
9. human and community review;
10. a preservation and maintenance plan.

**STEM serves heritage; heritage gives STEM human purpose.**

## Historical-account record

Every historical account should carry the following fields:

| Field | Requirement |
| --- | --- |
| **Record ID** | Stable identifier |
| **Title** | Clear human-readable name |
| **Narrator or creator** | Person or organization responsible for the source |
| **Interviewer or recorder** | Who captured the account |
| **Date and place** | When and where the source was created |
| **Source type** | Interview, photograph, letter, broadcast, artifact, website, ledger, or other form |
| **Original wording** | Verbatim quotation or unchanged source when available |
| **Summary** | Clearly labeled editorial description |
| **Consent and rights** | Recording, preservation, access, quotation, and publication permissions |
| **Evidence status** | Testimony, verified, corroborated, disputed, unverified, interpretation, canon, or fiction |
| **Provenance** | Origin, custody, file history, hashes, and revisions |
| **Related records** | People, places, events, organizations, and other sources |
| **Access level** | Public, restricted, confidential, embargoed, or private |
| **Human approval** | Name, decision, date, and exact version approved |
| **Preservation location** | Archive, repository, collection, and backup information |

## Evidence labels

- **SOURCE:** An original or directly attributable record.
- **TESTIMONY:** A first-person or attributed account.
- **CORROBORATED:** Supported by one or more independent sources.
- **VERIFIED:** Confirmed within a clearly stated scope.
- **DISPUTED:** Substantive sources conflict.
- **UNVERIFIED:** Not yet independently confirmed.
- **INTERPRETATION:** Meaning proposed by an analyst, editor, or community member.
- **CANON:** Human-declared project identity, governance, or creative continuity.
- **FICTION / WORLDBUILDING:** Imaginative narrative not presented as historical fact.
- **RESTRICTED:** Preserved but not authorized for public access.

## Safeguarding workflow

**Receive → Consent → Capture → Preserve original → Describe → Research → Label evidence → Narrator review → Human approval → Publish or restrict → Maintain and migrate**

A useful archive does not merely collect files. It protects context, rights, meaning, and continued accessibility.

## Ethical boundaries

- Communities and narrators help define what belongs to their living heritage.
- Public access does not cancel privacy, cultural sensitivity, copyright, or consent.
- Confidential ACIR material is not published by inference.
- Sacred or restricted knowledge remains restricted when its custodians require it.
- AI assistance must be disclosed where material and never substitutes for narrator or community authority.
- A polished narrative must not erase uncertainty, contradiction, or correction.
- UNESCO terminology may be cited; UNESCO affiliation or endorsement must not be implied.
- ACE Folklife Historical Society is identified here as a project designation unless separate legal status is independently documented.
